Air it out.

Five minutes each. One talks. The other plays it back until the speaker says "That's it." Then you switch.

The work nobody sees has four steps.

Sociologist Allison Daminger found that the hardest household work is mental: anticipating what's needed, finding options, deciding, then checking it actually happened. Most of it is invisible to the person who isn't doing it. Both of you carry some. Here's what usually goes unseen, on each side.

Anticipate

Seeing it coming

Often missed on her sideThe shoes are getting tight before anyone complains.

Often missed on his sideThe car needs tires before the first snow.

Identify

Finding the options

Often missed on her sideThree summer camps, every sign-up date, which ones fill in a day.

Often missed on his sidePricing the refinance at 11 PM so the numbers don't scare anyone at dinner.

Decide

Making the call

Often missed on her sideWhich birthday parties are worth a whole Saturday.

Often missed on his sideWhich work fire to absorb so he can make bedtime.

Monitor

Making sure it happened

Often missed on her sideChecking the camp form actually went through.

Often missed on his sideWatching how your kid is handling the new coach.

Framework: Allison Daminger, "The Cognitive Dimension of Household Labor," American Sociological Review, 2019. Examples are ours.

Five questions on the table.

What do you track that I've never once asked about?

What do I think I do that you actually finish?

When this week did you feel alone in this house?

What would you drop tomorrow if you knew I really had it?

What do you carry for me that you've never said out loud?

02

Sort it out. Inside Manny.

Manny already knows the jobs your house runs on, because he's been reading the school emails and the calendar. Tonight you each sort the same cards on your own phone: Mine, Yours, or Manny's. Then Manny shows you exactly where you disagree.

03

Fold it in.

You fold a real basket together, side by side, hands busy, eyes forward. It's the easiest place to keep talking. Before the last towel, you set your nightly check-in: same time, ten minutes, three questions.

What landed on you today? What can I take? What's tomorrow?

The kids aren't parked. They're in a lab.
