Your people have two jobs. You can only see one.

02 · Relics of the workday

Your workday was built for 1955. Your team lives in 2026.

Most of the workday was designed around a household with one earner and someone at home: at the school door at 3:00, home for the baby years, with Grandma down the street. That household changed. The workday didn't. Your double-booked people are covering the difference.

THE 40-HOUR WEEK

WHAT'S HAPPENING

Full-time jobs are still designed as if someone else is running the home.

WHY IT'S A RELIC

The 40-hour week became law in 1938. As late as 1970, only 31% of two-parent families had both parents working full time. Today it's 46%. In nearly half of those families, nobody is free to run the home during the workday.

Fair Labor Standards Act, 1938 · Pew Research Center, 2015

THE 3:00 BELL

WHAT'S HAPPENING

School lets out at 3:00. Your 4:30 meeting is still on.

WHY IT'S A RELIC

Most schools close two or more hours before the workday ends, plus 29 closure days a year outside summer. A worker with paid leave averages 16 days of vacation and holidays. Someone has to cover that gap, and it's usually a parent.

Center for American Progress, 2016

THE BABY WEEKS

WHAT'S HAPPENING

A new dad is back at his desk within two weeks.

WHY IT'S A RELIC

The federal leave law dates to 1993. It guarantees 12 weeks, unpaid, and only 56% of workers qualify. The U.S. is the only one of 41 nations studied with no paid leave for new parents. 7 in 10 dads take 10 days or less.

FMLA, 1993 · U.S. Dept. of Labor · Pew Research Center, 2019

THE VILLAGE

WHAT'S HAPPENING

"Can your mom grab the kids?"

WHY IT'S A RELIC

Grandma has a job too. Women 55 to 64 in the workforce went from 43% in 1970 to 60% in 2024. The backup plan is in a meeting. Or she wants to just be Grandma now. Or she's burnt out and needs to rest.

OECD data via FRED, St. Louis Fed

THE BUDGET

WHAT'S HAPPENING

"Just get a sitter."

WHY IT'S A RELIC

Child care averages $13,128 a year. For two kids in a center, it costs more than median rent in 49 states and DC. That's not a small ask.

Child Care Aware of America, 2024

THE DAD

WHAT'S HAPPENING

Dads who leave at 5:00 to be with their kids still pay for it at work.

WHY IT'S A RELIC

Dads now spend nearly three times as much time with their kids as in 1965: 7.3 hours a week, up from 2.5. Work schedules are still built as if they spent none. And dads who use flexible schedules can face a "flexibility stigma": being seen as less committed.

Pew Research Center, 2013 · U.S. Dept. of Labor

Until the workday catches up, your people pay the difference. And you know what? So do you.

In lost focus, slipping work, and your best people walking out the door.

03 · What it costs: the hidden price of the second calendar

Free their time to free their mind.

A parent with an unsolved pickup carries it into every meeting. Researchers call it attention residue: an unfinished task keeps pulling at you and drags down whatever you do next. For a double-booked person, home is the unfinished task that never closes.

Being double-booked means being needed in two places at once. Mental load is everything they keep track of in their heads all day. Fix the scheduling conflicts and you lighten the mental load too.

04 · The fix: the Modern Operator Standard

Be a Modern Operator.

Double-booked people don't need another training. They need permission to run home.

Modern Operators design the systems, write the policies and train the leaders for how people actually live now. Everyone else is running a 1955 playbook on 2026 families, and their double-booked people are paying for it. The Modern Operator Standard is five commitments, each one backed by research.

Are you a Modern Operator? Take the 2-minute quiz →

01

Let people work wherever the work can get done

If the work can be done from the kitchen table while a kid is home sick, let it be done there, without anyone asking permission.

Why: In a randomized trial of 1,612 employees, working from home two days a week cut quits by about a third, with no drop in performance reviews or promotions. Quits fell most among women.

02

Home is allowed at work

Booking the doctor and taking the school's call happen on company time, without anyone sneaking into a stairwell to do it.

Why: 47% of employees already say caregiving affects their work all or most of the time. The only choice is whether they handle it openly or in hiding.

03

Required time off, not "unlimited"

Replace "unlimited PTO" with a minimum people must take, and track it like a goal.

Why: "Unlimited" doesn't add rest. In payroll data from about 1,000 companies, people on unlimited plans took about 12 days a year, roughly the same as people on capped plans.

04

Pay for the mental load

A stipend for the AI tools and services that carry the home logistics, like Manny.

Why: Replacing one employee costs one-half to two times their salary. Support costs less than turnover.

05

Leaders run home first

Leaders put their own family time on the calendar, where the team can see it. When the boss does it, everyone else feels allowed to.

Why: Research on supervisors as work-life role models finds employees tend to follow the boundaries their managers keep.

Sources: Bloom, Han & Liang, randomized trial of hybrid work, Nature, 2024 · Fuller & Raman, The Caring Company, Harvard Business School, 2019 · Namely PTO data, 2021 · Gallup, 2019 · Koch & Binnewies, supervisors as work-life role models, 2015.

05 · Modern Operator manager training

Better care for caregivers.

Policy sets the rules. Managers decide whether anyone feels safe using them. Gallup finds managers account for up to 70% of the variance in employee engagement. And in field studies, training supervisors in family-supportive behavior was linked to better performance, satisfaction and commitment, and lower intent to quit.

THE 4 LEADERSHIP MINDSETS

Four ways to lead.

Each card shows a mindset two ways, with your people and with AI: what it looks like, and what it costs you or gets you. Most managers are stuck at Doing with both. The jump to Designing starts with two questions: What do they need? What do I need?

1 · YOUR OWN HANDS

Doing

"Faster if I just do it myself."

With People

What it looks like

You carry everything and react to whatever is loudest. Nobody knows what anyone is juggling, including you.

What it costs

Caregivers hide what they're carrying until they quit.

With AI

What it looks like

You do it by hand, or type a one-off request and take whatever comes back.

What it costs

Your company pays people to do work a machine could.

2 · YOUR KNOW-HOW, WRITTEN DOWN

Documenting

"Here's how this gets done."

With People

What it looks like

You write down what good looks like, so the work stops depending on you and nobody has to guess what you meant.

What it gets you

Caregivers can step away without the work stopping.

With AI

What it looks like

You save clear instructions and examples, so AI gets it right the second time and every time after.

What it gets you

Your company stops losing know-how every time someone leaves.

3 · THE RIGHT WORK TO THE RIGHT HANDS

Designing

"What do they need, and what do I need?"

With People

What it looks like

You ask what's really going on before you hand anything off, then design the work around real lives: the school pickup, the parent's appointments.

What it gets you

Caregivers get work that fits their lives, so they stay.

With AI

What it looks like

You redesign the workflow so AI carries the repeat work and people keep the judgment work.

What it gets you

Your company does more with the same team.

4 · WHAT'S WORTH DOING

Directing

"What actually matters here?"

With People

What it looks like

You set the why, protect your team's time, and run home first where they can see it. The system runs without you.

What it gets you

Caregivers see the boss run home and feel allowed to.

With AI

What it looks like

You decide what never to automate, and where a human has to stay in the loop.

What it gets you

Your company keeps people where judgment matters most.

The asking at the heart of Designing builds on Jeff Wetzler's Ask Approach: choose curiosity, make it safe, pose quality questions, listen to learn, reflect and reconnect.

WHAT MANAGERS PRACTICE

The Better Care for Caregivers Toolkit

Built on P.E.E.R.: People Engagement and Emotional Regulation, the framework I've used to coach leaders for years. Managers have to be thermometers, reading the temperature of fires at work and at home. PE before ER: they practice these skills before the emergency, live and out loud, on their own real situations.

H.O.L.D. Space

Halt. Ask an Open question. Listen and Loop back what you heard. Dignify their experience, even when you see it differently.

The P.O.V. Method

Pause before you respond. Open it up: ask an open question, then play back what you heard. Validate what they're feeling, even when you see it differently. See it from their point of view, practiced until it's automatic.

Deblur your language

Turn "whenever you get a chance" into a clear ask with a real deadline, so nobody has to guess what you meant.

Discern, decide, do

When something said in a meeting lands wrong: figure out what's going on, decide whether to respond now or later, then act.

Open the circle

Notice who is always in the room and who never is. Watch who gets the floor, who gets talked over, and who quietly carries the admin work. Then change it.

Staying steady

You can't read anyone else's temperature if yours is running high. Tools for keeping your own reaction in check when a hard conversation gets heated.

Includes M.O.D.E.S. by Dr. Raquel Martin, PhD
